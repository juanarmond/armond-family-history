## Prologue — the arithmetic of a single person

Count backwards and the numbers turn strange. You have two parents, four grandparents, eight great-grandparents,
sixteen the generation before that. Keep going and by the time you reach the people who crossed oceans — the
generation that stepped off wooden ships onto Brazilian sand in the 1700s and 1800s — the line has fanned out
into hundreds of separate lives, lived on islands and in mountain valleys and in forest towns that had not yet
been cut out of the trees. None of them knew the others. A Swiss stonemason's orphaned son in the Jura foothills
had no reason ever to hear the name of a woman drowning her rosaries in the churches of a small Azorean fishing
parish; a Portuguese officer's family in the gold highlands of Minas had no way of imagining a German-speaking
colonist family clearing stony lots in the Serra dos Órgãos. And yet every one of those separate lives was, without
knowing it, walking toward the same point. That point is you.

This is the story of how four rivers — four grandparents, and the hundreds of ancestors funnelling down behind each
of them — found their way to a single confluence. Two of the rivers rise in the Atlantic islands and the Portuguese
Minho; one rises in the Alpine cantons of Switzerland and the hills of Hessen; all four run down into the same
strip of Brazilian forest in the nineteenth century, the coffee frontier of the Zona da Mata Mineira, and are then
scattered again by a twentieth-century steel town. Where they finally braid together is Juan Armond. Let us travel
up each river to its source, and then come back down.

## RIVER ONE — the Armond, out of Terceira and the gold highlands (Geraldo Paz Armond)

### A house on a small island

Begin on the Ilha Terceira, in the Azores, in the middle of the seventeenth century — a green volcanic island in
the mid-Atlantic, its economy built on wheat sent to mainland Portugal, on the blue dye *pastel*, on wine and
ship's timber, its bishop's city of Angra a busy port on the sea-road to the Indies. In the rural parish of São
Sebastião, just outside Angra, a boy named **Gaspar de Souto Maior** was baptised on 23 December 1657 — we know
because his baptism was found and read on the open parish registers, and because his death entry, seventy years
later on 2 December 1727, gives his age as "setenta annos," a clean cross-check. On 2 October 1690, in the little
Igreja de Nossa Senhora da Graça, Gaspar married **Margarida das Candeias Machado**, herself baptised in that same
parish in May 1667. Their marriage act survives and names both their fathers — Gaspar's an ensign, *Alferes*
Belchior da Ponte Cardoso; Margarida's a man called Francisco Ferreira de **Ermonde**.

Hold onto that word, *Ermonde*. It is the whole surname's secret. The family that would one day be called
**Armond** in Brazil did not get its name from Gaspar's grand-sounding "Souto Maior" (an old kindred name on his
own side). It got it through Margarida — her father and her brother, "Alferes Manoel Machado Ermonde," already
carried it, and it was her son who took his mother's family name rather than his father's. Trace the *Ermonde*
line up through the held records and the well-sourced compilations and it runs back, generation by generation, to a
man remembered as **Francisco Ferreira Drummond** — *madeirense*, "tronco na Terceira," born around 1580, the
trunk from which the island branch grew. Above him, tradition holds that the name is a Portuguese softening of the
Scottish **Drummond**: Scotland to Madeira to the Azores to Brazil, a surname migrating across four centuries and
two oceans before it ever reached a Brazilian baptismal font. *Tradition holds* — no baptism, and certainly no
Y-DNA, yet proves the Scottish leap. It is the family's oldest and most romantic story, and it is honestly labelled
here as story, not fact.

Margarida died young, in April 1693. Her son, **Francisco Ferreira Armonde**, born in São Sebastião around 1691,
grew up on that finite, crowded island in exactly the decades when the Minas Gerais gold strike (from 1697) was
pulling Azoreans and Minhotos off their land so fast that the Crown imposed emigration controls in 1709 and again
in 1720 to stop the bleeding. Around 1725, Francisco and his brother Antônio joined that stream — younger men of a
gentry-adjacent island family, going to the mainland frontier for the land and fortune the island could not give
them, while their father Gaspar stayed and died on Terceira. It is the oldest and cleanest migration in the whole
tree: island land-hunger pushing, gold pulling.

### The gold road and the Borda do Campo

Francisco came ashore into colonial Minas Gerais and settled along the **Caminho Novo**, the royal road hacked from
Rio de Janeiro up to the mines and finished for pack-mules by 1707 — the artery down which gold flowed to the port
and up which everything else was carried. Along it grew **Barbacena**, first a great ranch called the Fazenda da
Borda do Campo, its parish of Nossa Senhora da Piedade erected in 1725, a supply-and-cattle post in the highland
"Campo das Vertentes." There Francisco Ferreira Armonde lived and died — at the Fazenda do Rio Fundo, on 16
November 1751. His will and inventory survive; he was illiterate and signed with a cross, and the document names
his long-dead island parents, *"Gaspar do Souto Maior e sua mulher Margarida das Candeias, já defuntos."* The first
Armond in Brazil could not write his own name, but he made sure the record remembered his mother's.

From this Barbacena trunk grew a family that climbed into the Empire's minor aristocracy: a **Barão de Pitangui**
(the ensign's grandson Marcelino, born 1782, first baron; then the second baron, Honório Augusto José Ferreira
Armond, 1819–1874, whose eleven children are all named in his 1874 Mar de Espanha inventory), a **Conde de Prados**
— Dr. Camillo Ferreira Armond, a physician trained at the University of Paris and a councillor of the Empire, whose
death "na Côrte" on 14 July 1882 drew a long obituary in the Leopoldina newspaper *O Leopoldinense*, calling him
"um dos mais illustres filhos desta provincia" — and even the family priests, like *padre* José Joaquim Ferreira
Armond, vigário of the Barbacena matriz in 1820, remembered in *O Pharol* as "tio do conde de Prados e do visconde
de Pitanguy." Generations later a descendant of the titled branch, **Geralda Ferreira Armond**, would direct the
Museu Mariano Procópio in Juiz de Fora, the twentieth-century keeper of the family's memory.

### The gap at the heart of the tree

And here is the honest fracture in the story — the mystery that sits at the very centre of who Juan is. Juan's own
direct Armond ancestor, **Simplício José Ferreira Armond**, was *not* one of the baron's eleven children. Every
newspaper and probate search — *O Leopoldinense*, *O Pharol*, the *Correio da Manhã*, the Full-Text corpus — has
been run to exhaustion, and Simplício appears nowhere in the titled line. He is, on all the evidence, a
**collateral Armond**: a real branch of the family, carrying the true patrilineal surname down his own father's
line, but whose father *we cannot yet name*. His parents are the single most important missing record in the entire
pedigree, the keystone. Until it is found, the whole glittering Terceira trunk — Gaspar, Margarida, the Ermonde
line, the Drummond tradition — attaches to Juan only by a bridge we have not yet proven. It is a documented family;
it is *probably* Juan's; but the marriage act that would clinch it, Simplício's wedding to Eliza around 1868–72 in
the little coffee-country parish of **Argirita / Bom Jesus do Rio Pardo**, was never microfilmed. It waits, off in
the world, in the Arquivo Histórico Arquidiocesano de Juiz de Fora (request 160/2026, somewhere in a long queue).
The tree's proudest branch hangs on the record we still lack.

### Simplício himself — the self-made man in the coffee towns

What we *do* know of Simplício is vivid. He lived his whole documented life, from about 1875 to after 1915, in the
Zona da Mata coffee towns — Leopoldina, Muriaé, Argirita — and he was manifestly a man who had made himself. He was
**literate** in a mostly illiterate world; his own handwriting survives in the 1879 inventory of D. Matilde, where
he signed *"Simplicio Joze Ferr.a Armond. Estou sciente."* He was a court **arbitrador** and **curador** (a
sworn estate-appraiser and guardian), still being ordered out to appraise a dead man's estate at Santa Rita da
Glória as late as July 1905; a **delegado** of Muriaé, noted in *O Pharol* around 1902 in his police capacity; a
**creditor** in an 1885 Leopoldina probate that was, tellingly, the estate of a coffee fazenda — placing him right
inside the credit economy of the frontier. On 27 July 1881, two months before he first appears in the record as
Eliza's husband, *O Leopoldinense* lists him in a syndicate buying into the "Grande Loteria da Côrte" alongside his
future brother-in-law **Ladisláo Egydio Ferreira de Toledo** and a cluster of other Toledos — a small, human scene
of an ambitious man knitting himself into a propertied family. He even, in another notarial act, signed *"a rogo da
outorgante por não saber escrever"* — on behalf of a woman who *couldn't* write. Simplício was the literate one in
the room.

But — and this is the analytical spine of the whole Armond arc — his standing was the kind that does *not* pass
down. Offices are income while you hold them, not heritable capital. The land and status in his household were
mostly his wife **Eliza's** Toledo inheritance, married into, not his own. And if he was, as the evidence suggests,
a natural or collateral Armond, he inherited nothing at all from the glittering trunk. So the family's fall was not
a lost fortune or a scandal; it was arithmetic. Brazilian law split every estate equally among all the children;
what little there was fragmented across Simplício and Eliza's children and again across *their* children. The
coffee economy the money sat inside collapsed after abolition and the price crashes. And the family urbanised —
which, in Brazil in the early twentieth century, meant arriving in the city as a wage-earner with no land. So
Simplício the literate arbitrador begat a line that, two generations on, produced **Geraldo Paz Armond** — a
**baker**, a *padeiro*, in a steel town, with little schooling and no land. That is not a mystery. It is the most
common Brazilian family arc of the century, and it quietly supports the reading of Simplício as a self-made
collateral: such a man leaves *status*, not capital, and status does not reproduce itself.

### Eliza's people — the landed Toledos who married out

If the Armond side is a decline, the Toledo side Simplício married into is a *dissolution* — a genuinely landed
line that dispersed itself. **Eliza Balbina de Toledo** was the granddaughter of the family's matriarch, **Matildes
Maria de Jesus**, who bought and held the **Fazenda da Concórdia** in the Rio Pardo / Leopoldina forest and died
there in 1879 — a slaveholding coffee seat in a district that by 1883 carried the *second-largest enslaved
population in all of Minas Gerais*. That is the weight of it, told plainly: the land Juan's family once held was
worked by enslaved people, and the wealth was coffee wealth. Eliza's parents were **José Cesário de Toledo Lima**
(baptised at São João del-Rei in 1813, dead by 1866) and **Claudina Brandina de Jesus**; her grandparents,
Matilde and **Ladisláo Egídio Ferreira de Toledo**, married at the São João del-Rei matriz on 8 May 1810. The
capital was real — and partible inheritance split the Concórdia across the thirteen-plus children of one generation
and the many children of the next, and Eliza herself married a self-made office-holder rather than another
landholder. The land dispersed; the standing married out. The Armond decline and the Toledo dissolution are the
*same event*, seen from two sides.

Behind Eliza, the Toledo line runs back cleanly, on held primary records, deep into the eighteenth-century gold
highlands and across the Atlantic to the Minho. Her great-grandfather, **Capitão Joaquim José Ferreira de Toledo**,
was a *morador na Villa Rica* — a resident of Ouro Preto, the gold capital — who married **Rita Angélica
Rodrigues** on 15 January 1783 at the Capela de Nossa Senhora da Glória in Barbacena, and whose 1833 inventory (he
the widower, Rita having died intestate on 9 February 1832) names all thirteen of their children together. Rita's
own baptism was found: 1764, at the **Capela de São José do Ribeirão**, a chapel-of-ease under Barbacena's mother
church — and it names *her* father, **João Rodrigues do Valle**, as *"natural da freguesia de São Martinho de
Ruivães, Arcebispado de Braga"*: a Minho man from the Lima valley. Another quarter of Eliza's ascent, the
**Silva-Xavier** line, is a small epic of frontier mobility on its own — Minho immigrant men marrying colonial
Paulista women: **Sargento Amaro da Silva Barreto**, natural of the town of **Guimarães** in Braga, married
**Perpétua da Silva** of the city of São Paulo; **João Rodrigues do Mello** of **Viana** in Braga married **Maria
Francisca Cordeira** of **Itu** in São Paulo — and their descendants rode the gold-and-cattle frontier from Goiás
(Meia Ponte) down through Paracatú to Barbacena, where a baptism in 1795 finds them settled beside the Armonds. So
even before the four rivers meet in Brazil, this one river is already a confluence in itself: the Azores, the
Minho, and colonial São Paulo, braided in the gold highlands.

### Aristão, Liliosa, and the blank called Paz

Down at the modern end of the Armond river stands **Aristão Ferreira Armond**, born around 1879 near Piacatuba in
the Mata, dead in Rio de Janeiro on 1 November 1957 — the generation that moved the family out of the dying coffee
country and toward the capital. He married **Liliosa Paz Armond**, and here the river runs into sand: the entire
**Paz** family is a total blank. Liliosa is firmly real — her name is typed, unmistakably, on a 1973 Guanabara
driver's-registry entry for her son José Olavo, and she died on 16 April 1946 in the Eugenópolis / Rosário da
Limeira district of the Mata — but her parents are unknown, and her documentary *silence* is itself a piece of
evidence: unlike the Toledo women a generation up, who surface again and again in probate paper precisely because
they held property, Liliosa appears in no inventory, no land record, nothing but her descendants' certificates. A
woman who leaves no estate trail is a woman with no land to divide. The record that would open her whole line — her
1946 Eugenópolis death certificate — has not yet been retrieved.

Their son **Geraldo Paz Armond** was born on 30 January 1915 at Limeira in the Mata, and died on 18 February 1991
at the Hospital Santa Margarida in **Volta Redonda** — the steel city — a retired *padeiro*. On 31 May 1952, at
Eugenópolis, he had married **Cidália Engrácio Guimarães**. And with that marriage, a second river joins the first.

## RIVER TWO — the Guimarães and the Engrácio, out of the Minho by way of commerce (Cidalia Engrácio Guimarães)

Cidalia's line is the shortest of the four and the one that runs *uphill*. It is the immigrant's story: arrive with
nothing, climb in a single generation, and hold the height only briefly.

Its Portuguese source is a man recorded simply as *portuguez* — **Vicente José de Carvalho Guimarães**. Everything
about the name points to the **Minho**, Portugal's most crowded and most land-starved region and the great source of
eighteenth- and nineteenth-century emigration to Brazil; "Carvalho Guimarães" is a Minho toponym almost certainly
fixed in Brazil, and the leading (still unproven) lead is a *mestre pedreiro* — a master mason — of the São Paio
parish in the town of Guimarães itself. Vicente did not sail straight to the Mata. He landed first on the **São
Paulo coffee frontier**, at Rio Claro, where his son was born and where his own marriage record still sits,
located but access-gated, waiting to name his Portuguese parish — the door to the Minho we have not yet opened.

His son, **Francisco José de Carvalho Guimarães**, is the high point of the arc. Born at Rio Claro, he turns up in
Carangola in 1897 as an **Alferes** — a second-lieutenant — of the Guarda Nacional, and the same militia roll lists
a whole cluster of "de Carvalho Guimarães" officers who are very likely his brothers. Immigrant to local notable in
one generation: militia rank in the interior was a marker of a propertied, literate, locally respected man, and an
immigrant's son reaching it is the signature of a family that *rose*. (The rank itself rests on a transcribed roll,
so it is a strong lead rather than a held certificate — but the shape of the climb is unmistakable.) Yet, like all
office-and-rank standing, it did not convert into heritable land. His daughter, **Maria Aurora Guimarães** (born
about 1904, died in Rio de Janeiro on 15 September 1991), married into modest urban commerce and ended her life
"do lar" in the capital.

The commerce came from the other half of Cidalia's line, the **Engrácio de Souza** family. **António Engrácio de
Souza** and **Luzia Pinheiro da Conceição** were the grandparents; their son **António Engrácio Filho** (born about
1894, died in Rio de Janeiro on 21 June 1964) was a **negociante** — a storekeeper — who followed the market from a
declining interior coffee town to the capital, a modest urban merchant whose position was tied to regional commerce
rather than to land. It is the "contingent commerce" arc: a living made from trade, which rises and falls with the
economy around it and does not accumulate into an estate.

Francisco's daughter Maria Aurora married António Engrácio Filho, and their daughter — born on 15 September 1930 at
**Alvorada**, in the Carangola district of the Mata — was **Cidália Engrácio Guimarães**, who would marry Geraldo
Armand and become Juan's paternal grandmother. Remember that birthplace, Alvorada; it is about to become the sharp
point of the whole story.

## RIVER THREE — the Muniz, out of the Azorean sea and the class floor (Antenor Muniz)

### A drowning off the Garajau

The third river rises on a different Azorean island — not Terceira but **São Miguel**, in the seaward parish of
**Nossa Senhora da Mãe de Deus** in the Povoação district, on the island's rugged eastern end. This is the family
that anchors the whole tree's *baseline* — not gentry, not merchants, but small landholding peasant farmers,
*proprietários*, living close enough to the sea that the sea took the patriarch.

**Manoel Muniz Byttancourt** was baptised at Mãe de Deus on 17 June 1821 — his baptism is held and read. On 17
February 1842, after the three banns and a dispensation from a *fourth-degree consanguinity impediment* (he and his
bride were blood cousins, an ordinary fact of life inside a small island parish where everyone was related to
everyone), he married **Francisca Roza do Espírito Santo**. And on 24 July 1866, around one o'clock in the
afternoon, Manoel drowned — his death record N°70 reads that he died *"na bahia, fora do Garajau,"* in the bay
beyond the Garajau, *"sem receber os Sacramentos,"* leaving eight children and no will, a resident of the Lomba do
Botão. A fisherman-farmer's death, in the water off his own coast, at forty-five.

Behind Manoel the São Miguel peasant line now runs two generations deeper still, all of it on primary parish ink
recovered through the archive at Ponta Delgada: his parents **João Francisco Muniz Byttancourt** and **Maria
Jacintha de Medeiros**, married at Mãe de Deus on 7 October 1819; and *their* parents, the elder **Manoel Muniz
Bitancourt × Tereza do Amaral** and **João de Medeiros Brandão × Maria Eugenia**, whose own marriages predate 1801
and sit, still, in undigitized books at the archive. This line, unlike the Armonds, *never rose and never fell*. It
is the rural working baseline of the whole tree — which is exactly why, generations later, it could intermarry with
a falling gentry line. They met in the middle.

### The crossing — two brothers to Brazil

Manoel and Francisca's children included **twin boys**, João and Manoel, baptised together on 24 March 1845. One of
those twins, **João Muniz Bittencourt Jr.** — his own record confirming him *"natural da Ilha de São Miguel,
Portugal"* — crossed the Atlantic sometime before 1875 (he left no departure passport, and the São Miguel passport
registers only begin that year), arriving around 1866–70 as a coffee *colono* in the post-abolition-era labour wave.
His likely brother José crossed in the same window; two brothers emigrating together fits the record. They landed
first at **Santo Antônio de Sapucaia**, in the Paraíba valley of Rio de Janeiro province, and then pushed up into
the **Zona da Mata** — the same forest frontier, from the far side, that the Armond and Toledo lines were coming
down into.

In Sapucaia, João Jr. married **Suzana Rita Brandão** — the bishop's marriage provision was issued on 23 December
1882 — and it is worth pausing on Suzana, because her family, the **Rego Brandão**, is the tree's small emblem of
*rootedness*: a settled family whose baptism, marriage, and death records cluster tidily in one place, generation on
generation. Suzana was born on 20 August 1865 and baptised at Sapucaia on 22 March 1866, daughter of **José do Rego
Brandão** and **Rita Ignacia de Jesus**; her maternal grandfather Antonio Caetano Machado even stood as her
godfather, the kind of dense local kinship that marks a family staying put. João Jr. himself died far up in the
Mata, on 12 September 1915, at the Corrego do Boa Vista in Carangola, leaving eleven children — the São Miguel
fisherman's grandson, ending as a Carangola farmer.

### The invisible poor — the Azevedo

João Jr. and Suzana's son **Deocleciano Muniz Bittencourt**, born at Sapucaia on 1 June 1892, married **Luiza
Fernandes de Azevedo** at Santa Luzia de Carangola on 21 October 1916. And Luiza's line, the **Azevedo**, is the
floor of the whole class range in Juan's tree — so near-invisible in the record that their invisibility *is* the
evidence. Luiza's parents were **José Secundino de Azevedo** and **Thereza Fernandes de Azevedo**, and beyond their
names almost nothing survives: no land record, no estate, no digital footprint at all. We know a sister, **Gloria
Lacerda de Azevedo**, died in Niterói in 1900, which places the family briefly in the RJ orbit; a namesake's death
record hints, only hints, at a possible origin in **Madeira** (the parish of Nossa Senhora dos Prazeres, Calheta).
But the Azevedo grandparents — Secundino's and Thereza's own parents — are simply a blank, a whole couple-pair of
Juan's ancestors whose names are unrecovered. A family that leaves no paper is, almost always, a family that owned
nothing worth writing down. In the tree's range from a Conde de Prados to the Azevedo poor, this is the far pole.

Deocleciano and Luiza's son was **Antenor Muniz**, born on 2 November 1923 at **Alvorada**, in Carangola — and
documented, in 1949, simply as a man *"da lavoura,"* of the land: a farmer, exactly where his São Miguel
great-great-grandfather had been, the baseline unbroken across a century and an ocean. On 7 December 1949, at the
Vila de Alvorada, Antenor married **Iris Bohrer**. And with that, the fourth river arrives — the one that comes not
from the Atlantic islands but from the Alps.

## RIVER FOUR — the Bohrer, out of Switzerland and Hessen (Iris Bohrer)

### The stony lots of Nova Friburgo

The fourth river is the strangest to find at the confluence, because it begins in cold mountain country a world away
from the Azores. In 1819, King João VI — the Portuguese court then resident in Rio, having fled Napoleon — launched
a scheme to plant a colony of Swiss and German Catholic farmers in the highlands above Rio de Janeiro, in the Serra
dos Órgãos, at a place they named **Nova Friburgo**, after the Swiss canton of Fribourg. It was a brutal beginning.
The Atlantic crossing killed roughly a quarter of the colonists; the survivors were handed small, stony, steep lots
and left to make farms of them. This is the *poorest possible footing* in the whole tree — and, because it was so
low, the only direction it could go was up.

Two Borer men came off that fleet, from the canton of **Solothurn** in the Swiss Jura foothills, and Iris descends
from *both* of them — her father's line from one, her mother's from the other. **Laurent (Lorenz) Borer**, born at
Grindel on 27 February 1797, arrived at Nova Friburgo in 1819 aboard the ship *Heureux Voyage* and was assigned
House 73, Lot 86; he married **Anna Maria Wehrli**, of the same Grindel parish, in 1820. Her father, **Johann Jacob
Wehrli**, born at Grindel on 17 January 1751, had married **Elisabetha Borer** — baptised at Erschwil on 17 June
1760 — in the Grindel Catholic church on 27 January 1782, an entry read verbatim off the surviving Swiss register:
*"Die 27ma Januarii 1782: Jacobus Wehrlia × Elisabetha Borer, ambo ex Grindel, Erischwyg."* The other Borer,
**Jacob Borer**, born at Erschwil on 11 May 1801, came to Nova Friburgo as an **orphan** — his parents had died in
Switzerland before the voyage — and married **Marie Catharine Moser**, of Hägendorf, at the Nova Friburgo cathedral
in 1825. Into this Swiss pool married German-speaking Hessen families too — the **Klein** from Becherbach, the
**Schenkel** from Ottzweiler, the **Rutschmann** and **Storck** — who had come on the ship *Argus* in 1824. The
colony intermarried so tightly (two Borer brothers married two Wehrli sisters) that its family tree looks like a
lattice.

### From colonists to comerciantes

And then the line climbed. Jacob's son **Francisco José Borer** (born 4 July 1832, died 6 July 1888 at Nova
Friburgo) married **Rosa Eugenia de Lemos**, a Portuguese-descended woman from Itaboraí whose long-standing union
was only judicially registered in 1879 as a *casamento justificado* — the couple had, the witnesses swore, been
married *"a mais de vinte annos,"* since about 1858 or earlier. Laurent's grandson **Valentim Martinho Bohrer**
(born 14 November 1868) married **Carolina Schuenck Klein**, carrying the German-Hessen strand into the line. By the
1890s the family had risen from colonist farmers into local politics and even journalism: **Guilherme Samuel
Bohrer**, a brother of the line, founded a Nova Friburgo newspaper, *A Sentinella*, on 30 September 1894, and
served as a *Capitão* and Subdelegado of the São Pedro district — the colonist's grandson become a small-town
notable, his family's births and birthdays printed in his own paper's social columns. The climb continues to the
last generation on Swiss soil's memory: **João Gonçalves Bohrer**, born around 1897 at Nova Friburgo, ended his
life as a **comerciante** on a named street in **Volta Redonda**, dying on 3 August 1970 in the hospital of the
Companhia Siderúrgica Nacional — the steel company itself. He had married **Celina Bohrer** (born about 1900, died
15 February 1977 in Volta Redonda, a *costureira* — a seamstress), and — the lattice again — she too was a Borer, of
the Erschwil trunk, so that Iris's father and mother were both Bohrers of two different Swiss immigrant lines. Upward
mobility off a floor so low it could only rise: that is the Bohrer arc, the mirror-image of the Armond fall.

João Gonçalves and Celina had fifteen children. One of them, born on 27 February 1929 at Presidente Soares in the
Alto Jequitibá of Minas Gerais, was **Iris Bohrer** — Juan's maternal grandmother, the Swiss-Alpine river's mouth.

## THE CONFLUENCE — where four rivers become one family

Now bring the rivers down to where they meet, and watch how tight the geography becomes.

Every one of the four lines, coming from Terceira and the Minho and São Miguel and the Swiss Jura, passes through
the *same strip of Brazilian forest* — the **Zona da Mata Mineira**, the nineteenth-century coffee frontier that
opened up when the Crown's old prohibition on settling the eastern "Sertões do Leste" finally lapsed and fertile
virgin forest, close to the Rio export market, drew families from every direction. The Armond and Toledo came *down*
into it from the exhausted gold highlands (Leopoldina, Muriaé, Argirita). The Muniz and the Bohrer came *up* into it
from the coast and the colony (Carangola, Presidente Soares). The Guimarães and Engrácio followed commerce into it
(Carangola, Alvorada). The coffee frontier is not a backdrop to this family; it is the literal ground on which the
four separate streams first flowed into the same valley.

And then the point narrows almost past belief. Of all the towns in that vast forest, **both of Juan's grandparent
couples tie to one tiny Carangola district: Alvorada.** Cidalia Engrácio Guimarães was *born* at Alvorada, in 1930.
Antenor Muniz was a *farmer* at Alvorada. Iris Bohrer *married* Antenor at Alvorada, in 1949. Three of the four
rivers physically touch the same speck on the map. This is the sharpest single geographic fact in the whole
dataset: the improbable funnel that took people off Azorean cliffs and Swiss mountainsides and out of Portuguese
market towns, and set them down, within a generation or two of each other, in the same handful of Zona-da-Mata
streets.

Then the twentieth century pulls them apart again — and, in doing so, joins them for good. Getúlio Vargas decreed
Brazil's first great steelworks, the **Companhia Siderúrgica Nacional**, in 1941, and the sleepy dairy town of
**Volta Redonda** exploded from under three thousand people to nearly thirty-six thousand by 1950, a planned steel
city sucking labour out of the surrounding Minas and Paraíba valley. The falling Armond line and the rising Bohrer
line both washed up there — Geraldo Armond the baker died in Volta Redonda; João Gonçalves Bohrer the comerciante
died in the CSN's own hospital. The coffee bust had emptied the countryside; partible inheritance had fragmented
what land there was; the steel city gathered the pieces into a working class. It is, all of it, the single most
common Brazilian family story of the century — and here it is running under all four of Juan's lines at once.

Out of that convergence come the two marriages that make Juan's parents. On the paternal side, **Geraldo Paz Armond
married Cidália Engrácio Guimarães** at Eugenópolis on 31 May 1952 — the falling Armond-Toledo gentry joined to the
climbing Guimarães and the trading Engrácio. On the maternal side, **Antenor Muniz married Iris Bohrer** at Alvorada
on 7 December 1949 — the flat São Miguel peasant baseline (with the near-invisible Azevedo poor folded in) joined to
the rising Swiss-colonist Bohrer. Two vectors on each side; two marriages; and their children — Juan's father,
**Luis Carlos** Armand, son of Geraldo and Cidalia; Juan's mother, **Lucinea**, daughter of Antenor and Iris, both
living, and so drawn here only in outline — are the two channels through which all four rivers, at last, run into
one bed.

And that bed is Juan.

## THE MEANING — why it had to be a convergence, not a lineage

Step back from the individual scenes and the shape of the answer is clear. Juan did not descend from *a* people. He
is the point where several very different peoples, moving for entirely unrelated reasons, happened to intersect.

Look at what had to be true. A titled-adjacent Portuguese-Azorean gentry line had to *fall* — through non-heritable
office income, partible inheritance, the coffee collapse, and urbanisation — from the world of the Conde de Prados
and the Fazenda da Concórdia all the way down to a baker in a steel town. A dirt-poor Swiss-and-German immigrant
colony line had to *rise* — off the deadly, stony lots of Nova Friburgo — through colonist farming and small-town
commerce up to a comerciante in that same steel town. A São Miguel peasant line had to hold *flat*, never rising and
never falling, from a fisherman drowned off the Garajau to a farmer at Alvorada, carrying an all-but-invisible poor
Azevedo strand along with it. And a self-made Minho-immigrant commercial line had to make its one-generation *climb*
into local standing and then settle into modest urban trade. Two of those trajectories go up, one goes down, one
stays level — and they could only produce Juan if they *all arrived at the same class level in the same place at the
same time*. The falling gentry had to fall exactly as far as the rising immigrants rose, and the flat peasants had
to be right there in the middle, for a Muniz-Bohrer daughter to marry an Armand-Engrácio son. The class arithmetic
of four separate centuries had to sum to one number. It did.

The throughlines are worth naming, because they recur across lines that never knew each other. **Migration** is the
constant engine — Azorean land-hunger and the gold pull; Minhoto emigration; the King's Swiss colony; the coffee
frontier; the Vargas steel city — every ancestor in this book is, at some point, a person leaving a place that could
not hold them. **Land versus office** is the constant fault line — the Toledo who held heritable land dissolved
slower than the Armond who held only office, but both dissolved, because in Brazil an estate divided among all the
children cannot survive two generations intact. **Rootedness versus mobility** plays out as a spectrum — the Rego
Brandão and the São Miguel Muniz stayed put and left tidy clustered records; the Armond and the Bohrer moved and
moved again and left records scattered across archives on two continents. And the sheer **class range** is
staggering for one person to contain: a Conde de Prados trained in Paris and a slaveholding coffee fazenda at one
end; the Azevedo poor who left no paper at all, and a fisherman who drowned without the sacraments, at the other.
Juan is the confluence not only of continents but of the whole social scale of a colonial and imperial world.

That is how all these people, from different countries and different centuries, ended up as one person. Not by a
single bloodline running true, but by a convergence — hundreds of ancestors on the Atlantic islands, in mainland
Portugal, in the Swiss and German uplands, and across the Brazilian frontier, funnelling down through the coffee
country and the steel city until, at a common working-class level in mid-century Minas and Rio, the four rivers met.
The macro-history did the assembling. The documents let us watch it happen. And the last name — carried, improbably,
all the way from a Scottish tradition through Madeira and Terceira and Barbacena and the Mata and Volta Redonda —
came to rest, at the end of all that motion, on Juan Armond.

## The threads still being pulled — the story is not finished

This is a true history, but it is an *open* one. Several of the rivers still disappear underground before we can
follow them to their source, and each of those gaps is a real record, somewhere in the world, not yet in our hands.
They are part of the story too — the questions the family is still asking.

1. **Simplício's parents — the keystone.** Who was the father of Simplício José Ferreira Armond? Until we know, the
   whole Terceira trunk — Gaspar, Margarida, the Ermonde/Drummond line back to ~1580 — is Juan's *probable* but
   *unproven* ancestry. The decisive record is Simplício's ~1868–72 marriage to Eliza at Argirita / Bom Jesus do Rio
   Pardo, never microfilmed, waiting in the Juiz de Fora archdiocesan archive. This is the single most valuable
   missing document in the tree.

2. **Francisca Roza's Azorean parents.** Francisca Roza do Espírito Santo's own baptism is a bounded blank across the
   surviving Mãe de Deus books — she was born before 1818, in registers still undigitized at the BPARPD archive in
   Ponta Delgada. The São Miguel river runs one generation further than we can yet see.

3. **The whole Paz branch.** Liliosa Paz Armond is proven to exist and proven to have died at Eugenópolis in 1946,
   but her parents — the entire Paz family — are unknown. Her 1946 death certificate would open the line at a stroke.

4. **The Azevedo grandparents.** Secundino and Thereza Fernandes de Azevedo's own parents are a total blank — a whole
   couple-pair of Juan's ancestors at the poorest end of the tree, chased now through Leopoldina court records and a
   faint, unconfirmed lead toward Madeira.

5. **The Guimarães origin in the Minho.** Vicente José de Carvalho Guimarães is recorded only as *portuguez*. His
   marriage record at Rio Claro, São Paulo — located but not yet read — should finally name the Portuguese parish he
   came from, and swing open the door to the Minho.

The four rivers are mapped almost to their headwaters. Five of the springs are still hidden. When they surface — and
in this kind of work they do surface, as archives digitise and letters are answered and certificates arrive — this
story will grow another chapter, further upstream, closer still to the beginning of how Juan came to be.
